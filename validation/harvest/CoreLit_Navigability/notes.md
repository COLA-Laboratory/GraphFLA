## FDC

The definition-setting Jones & Forrest paper uses the product-moment correlation of fitness and distance to the nearest global maximum. In §3, p. 2, its equation is `r = c_FD/(s_F s_D)`, where `c_FD` is the covariance and `s_F`, `s_D` are standard deviations; the same section specifies Hamming distance to the closest global maximum. This is why a Pearson call is the comparable GraphFLA option. GraphFLA’s `fdc` defaults to Spearman, but accepts Pearson: [`analysis/correlation.py`](../../../../GitHub/GraphFLA/graphfla/analysis/correlation.py:105).

The empirical target is Rowe et al.’s §3.8 result, stored in `record.json` with its short exact value quote and locator. Section 2, Materials and Methods, specifies a starting population of “4^10” sequences; this is over the assignment’s size threshold by arithmetic. The article describes FDC as correlation with distance to the nearest goal and treats the global optimum as the goal when known. This definition is compatible if `method="pearson"` is selected. The input is not currently obtainable: the paper’s conclusion points to `http://dbkgroup.org/direvol.htm`, but both HTTP and HTTPS now return 404; an Internet Archive CDX lookup returned 503. The source file was not rebuilt and no figure value was read.

## Random-walk autocorrelation

Weinberger (1990) is the cited origin; Stadler (1996) clarifies that landscape correlation can mean a time series sampled along a random walk or a distance-partition correlation. Rowe et al. §3.5 gives the operational random-walk definition. Its exact short fragment is “expected correlation … during a random walk”; the locator and GraphFLA-side definition are in the autocorrelation overlap in `record.json`. GraphFLA returns one lag-specific correlation pooled across its sampled walks, as stated in [`analysis/ruggedness.py`](../../../../GitHub/GraphFLA/graphfla/analysis/ruggedness.py:46).

Rowe et al. explicitly print an estimate of the decay length, not a lag-one autocorrelation coefficient. The value “4.5” appears in prose that says it is estimated from Figure 2. I recorded it as `printed`, without reading a plotted point. The correlation-length parameter τ is not the scalar returned by GraphFLA’s lag-specific `autocorrelation`; the metric comparison is definition-incompatible. The raw landscape file is also unavailable at the article’s source URL.

## Neutrality

Kimura’s neutral theory is foundational but does not define GraphFLA’s neighborhood-fraction statistic. His 1968 abstract says: “Calculating the rate of evolution in terms of nucleotide substitutions seems to give a value so high that many of the mutations involved must be neutral ones.” Locator: *Nature*, p. 624, Abstract.

Van Nimwegen et al. operationalize mutational robustness on an RNA folding neutral network. The paper’s abstract says the “average number of neutral mutant neighbors … [is given by the] matrix spectral radius.” Its RNA example is a simulated RNA structure network, not an empirically measured genotype-fitness array. The paper’s local numeric network-neutrality result is in the record with its Results locator; it is an average degree, not a fraction of all neighbors. GraphFLA instead defines `neutrality` as the proportion of neighbors whose fitness values fall within a threshold ([`analysis/robustness.py`](../../../../GitHub/GraphFLA/graphfla/analysis/robustness.py:157)). Those are different quantities. The family-level empirical screen is recorded separately as `no_overlap`.

Schuster et al.’s RNA folding work says that “the sequence space is percolated by extensive neutral networks connecting nearest neighbours folding into identical structures” (1994 abstract; PubMed). Fontana & Schuster state that “phenotypes denote equivalence classes of genotypes” (1998 abstract). These are computational genotype-phenotype maps, not measured empirical fitness surfaces. I found no printed, definition-matched neutrality fraction on an obtainable real landscape above the assignment’s size threshold. The distinction and the modeled-network numerical result are recorded in the `neutrality` overlap.

## Global-optimum access, path length, and distance

Kauffman & Levin’s abstract describes adaptive walks “via fitter neighbors, to locally or globally optimal entities.” Franke et al. give a commonly used accessible-path definition: “A mutational pathway is considered selectively accessible … if the fitness values encountered along it are monotonically increasing.” They restrict their analysis to shortest paths. GraphFLA’s `global_optima_accessibility` instead returns the fraction of configurations that can reach the single global optimum by any monotonic improving path ([`analysis/navigability.py`](../../../../GitHub/GraphFLA/graphfla/analysis/navigability.py:120)). Their concepts are related, but the endpoint, starting-set denominator, and—in Franke’s case—shortest-path restriction must be checked before a numeric comparison. Franke et al. describe their empirical input as an “8-locus data set for A. niger” (Figure 1 caption), below the requested size cutoff.

Aguilar-Rodríguez et al. define peak accessibility as a fraction of shortest accessible paths to the highest-affinity site. Figure 2c displays the landscape-level values as plotted symbols and error bars; I did not read any point. The article says its PBM data came from UniPROBE and CIS-BP and that the analyzed data are in public repositories. The printed results in the article are corpus summaries, not a printed single-landscape GraphFLA global-accessibility value. The publisher’s Supplementary Table 1 download returned an HTTP 200 HTML security page rather than a workbook; no landscape mapping or raw matrices were acquired for a corpus run.

The Wu paper prints “34% to 93%” for genotypes with paths to “all 15 fitness peaks.” That is an intersection over multiple peaks, while GraphFLA’s named metric targets one global optimum. The prior Wu dossier separately contains a custom all-15 ancestor-set calculation that rounds to the paper’s reported precision; it is not a result from `global_optima_accessibility`, so the record marks this candidate `definition_incompatible`.

Papkou et al. print “76.5%” for adaptive walks reaching a high-fitness peak. This is a walk-level simulation statistic; GraphFLA counts graph configurations. Their mean path-length value is averaged over variants and high-fitness peaks (Figure 3D caption), rather than paths to one global optimum. Rowe et al.’s printed mean distance in Table 3 is distance to local optima, not the global optimum. Those three divergences are recorded with each overlap’s paper quote and GraphFLA definition in `record.json`.

## Basin-fitness correlation

Rowe et al. assign points to peaks under a steepest-ascent hill-climber and state only a qualitative negative relationship between peak height and basin size; no numeric correlation coefficient or method is printed. The basin construction is conceptually close to GraphFLA’s greedy assignment, but there is no numeric target and the input file is unavailable.

Papkou et al. print a Spearman basin-fitness correlation. Their basin is the set of all variants from which a peak is accessible via an increasing path. GraphFLA’s `basin_fitness_correlation` uses `size_basin_greedy`, which assigns each genotype to a deterministic greedy basin ([`analysis/correlation.py`](../../../../GitHub/GraphFLA/graphfla/analysis/correlation.py:205)). A genotype can reach more than one Papkou peak, but belongs to one greedy basin in GraphFLA, so the correlation definitions differ. No run was made for that coefficient.

## Extradimensional bypass

The earliest explicit operational use located is Conrad (1990), p. 68: “We will call this upward-running pathway an extradimensional bypass, since the peaks remain isolated except in one of the newly added dimensions.” This defines an uphill route opened by adding a dimension; it does not define a percentage denominator. Cariani’s 2002 article revisits the term. Gavrilets’ 1997 “holey adaptive landscapes” is related theory, not the term’s explicit coining passage.

GraphFLA’s function counts the fraction of reciprocal-sign-epistasis type-19 motifs for which an accessible bypass exists ([`analysis/epistasis/motifs.py`](../../../../GitHub/GraphFLA/graphfla/analysis/epistasis/motifs.py:387)). Wu et al. print separate conversion-bypass and detour-bypass success rates for sampled reciprocal-sign interactions. The mechanisms and denominator differ from GraphFLA’s motif-level proportion. Their publisher supplements were obtainable, but no GraphFLA bypass calculation was run because this is definition-incompatible.

## Local optima and local-optima ratio

The paper definitions directly support the two successful peak-count reproductions:

- Wu et al., Methods, define a peak as a node with “0 out-degree.” The Results section prints “30 fitness peaks.” GraphFLA’s `n_lo` counts distinct local optima and collapses each local-optimum plateau once ([`landscape/_optima.py`](../../../../GitHub/GraphFLA/graphfla/landscape/_optima.py:7)). On this surface, the direct zero-outdegree count and GraphFLA’s plateau-aware `n_lo` both match the paper.
- Papkou et al. define peaks as variants with “higher fitness than all their one-mutant neighbors” and print the peak count in the ruggedness section. The existing curated component input has the paper-reported vertex count; GraphFLA returned the same `n_lo`. The prior Papkou dossier notes an unresolved difference between the whole-experiment published measured count and author archive rows. That source-count gap is separate from the reproduced largest component and remains open.
- Rowe et al. print the local-optimum count, but the input file could not be recovered from the paper-listed source URL. The paper uses a hill-climber that stops when no neighbor has higher fitness; exact ties/plateaus cannot be checked without the input, so the definition match is marked unknown.
- Aguilar-Rodríguez et al. define peaks including possible plateau peaks and print a range and a multi-peak corpus count. That result is across the full PBM corpus, not a named TF’s individual `n_lo`. The raw data are described as public, but the Supplementary Table 1 mapping could not be retrieved, so the aggregate run is recorded as input unavailable.

GraphFLA defines `local_optima_ratio` as `n_lo / n_configs` ([`analysis/ruggedness.py`](../../../../GitHub/GraphFLA/graphfla/analysis/ruggedness.py:20)). The reviewed papers report peak counts or other summaries; they do not print that ratio. As required, the record quote is exactly **NOT STATED IN PAPER**. No ratio was derived from a printed count and mislabeled as a reproduction.

## Acquisition and failure audit

- The Wu main PDF and both publisher XLSX supplements were retrieved with HTTP 200 and SHA-256 values in the acquisition log. The measured and imputed rows were combined read-only. The GraphFLA command followed the requested `PYTHONDONTWRITEBYTECODE=1 PYTHONPATH=... python script.py` form; `MPLCONFIGDIR` was directed into this validation workspace.
- The Papkou PDF and existing component CSV were reused from the already-reviewed `papers/Papkou2023` dossier / GraphFLA data directory; neither was overwritten. A fresh read-only GraphFLA call returned the printed peak count.
- The Rowe HTML full text was obtained from PMC. The paper says its data are available at `http://dbkgroup.org/direvol.htm`; today that endpoint and the HTTPS version returned 404. The Internet Archive CDX lookup returned 503. FDC and peak-count calculations were therefore not attempted.
- A PMC Van Nimwegen PDF endpoint returned HTTP 200 but a small HTML challenge body. It is retained under its `_codex` filename and explicitly excluded as a source. A valid SFI working-paper copy and full-text PMC article were used instead.
- Nature’s supplementary Table 1 route also returned HTTP 200 with an HTML security challenge, not an XLSX. This prevents a corpus-wide Aguilar-Rodríguez input reconstruction; the article PDF and supplementary-information PDF are retained.
- Process-scope exception: temporary `pdftotext` extracts were written to `/tmp` during source inspection and then removed. One earlier GraphFLA invocation also created a Matplotlib temporary cache outside the validation directory; subsequent runs set `MPLCONFIGDIR` inside validation. No file under the GraphFLA repository was modified, and every GraphFLA invocation set `PYTHONDONTWRITEBYTECODE=1`.
